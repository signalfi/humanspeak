# Why Invoice Approvals Stall in the Last Week of the Month, and How to Reroute Them

Every controller knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a steady pace. Then the calendar hits the 24th or 25th, and the queue swells. Plant managers are buried in production targets. Purchasing is reconciling receipts. Someone in maintenance has a stack of MRO invoices sitting in their inbox, unopened. By the time the last approval lands, your team is booking accruals on estimates and pushing the close into day six or seven.

It's tempting to blame the approvers. But in most 200-to-500-person manufacturers, the month-end stall is a routing problem. The way invoices are sent for approval turns a steady flow into a bottleneck at exactly the moment you can least afford one.

## Why the queue backs up when it does

**Approvers batch their work.** Operations leaders don't approve invoices as they arrive. They approve when prompted, and the strongest prompt is an AP clerk saying "we need these for close." That makes month-end the natural moment for approvals, so a month's worth of decisions gets compressed into a few days.

**Routing is sequential when it doesn't need to be.** Many approval chains still run one person at a time: department head, then plant manager, then controller, then CFO above a threshold. If any one of them is on the floor, traveling, or out sick, everything behind them waits. Four approvers who each take two days add up to more than a week.

**Three-way match exceptions pile up late.** In manufacturing, the invoice is often ready before the receipt is. Raw materials arrive and the receiving log doesn't get updated until someone chases it. Freight invoices land with accessorial charges nobody expected. Price variances on commodity inputs trip the match. These exceptions accumulate all month and surface at close, when AP finally has time to work them.

**Thresholds were set years ago and never revisited.** An approval limit of $5,000 might have made sense when the company had half its current revenue. Today it sends routine consumables and repeat supplier invoices to senior approvers who have better things to do.

## What to change in your approval routing

None of these fixes requires reorganizing your finance team. They are configuration decisions, and most can be made in a single working session with your AP lead and a few operations stakeholders.

### 1. Auto-approve invoices that already match

If an invoice matches the purchase order and the receipt within a defined tolerance, the purchasing decision has already been approved. A second human sign-off adds delay without adding control. Set tolerances by category, for example tighter on raw materials and looser on low-dollar consumables, and let matched invoices flow straight to payment scheduling. That frees your approvers to focus on the exceptions that actually need judgment.

### 2. Route in parallel, not in sequence

Where more than one approver is required, send the invoice to all of them at once and require each to sign off, rather than passing it down a chain. The controls are the same, since everyone still approves, but the elapsed time drops to the slowest single approver rather than the sum of all of them.

### 3. Rebuild your thresholds around dollar value and risk

Pull the last twelve months of invoices and look at where approvals actually cluster. You'll usually find that a large share of invoice volume represents a small share of spend. Raise the thresholds for recurring, contracted, low-risk spend, and keep senior review for large, unusual, or non-PO invoices. Document the rationale so your auditors see a deliberate policy, not a loosening of controls.

### 4. Make delegation automatic

Every approver should have a named backup, and the system should reroute to that backup when the primary hasn't acted within a set window, not only when someone remembers to set an out-of-office rule. For plant managers who spend most of their day away from a desk, mobile approval matters more than almost anything else on this list.

### 5. Fix receiving before you fix AP

If three-way match exceptions are your biggest month-end drag, the root cause usually sits on the receiving dock. Work with operations to set a same-day or next-day expectation for logging receipts, and give receiving staff a simple way to do it. A cleaner receiving log shrinks the exception queue at its source.

### 6. Set escalation SLAs and publish the results

Define how long an invoice may sit with an approver, perhaps 48 hours, before it escalates. Then share a simple monthly report showing average approval time by approver or department. Most managers don't realize they're the bottleneck until they see the number next to their name.

### 7. Move the effective cutoff earlier

Run a soft cutoff around the 20th for invoices received to date, with a push to clear anything aging past your SLA. Whatever arrives after that is handled through a smaller, more predictable final sweep and a disciplined accrual process, instead of a last-minute scramble.

## What changes when routing is fixed

The benefit isn't only a faster close. When approvals flow steadily through the month, you book fewer accruals on estimates, so there is less true-up work the following month. Early-payment discounts become capturable again, because invoices are approved inside the discount window. Supplier relationships improve, which matters when you're negotiating lead times on critical components. And your AP team spends close week closing, not chasing.

## Where to start

You don't need to fix all seven at once. Start with the data. Pull a report of every invoice approved in the last week of each month over the past two quarters, and look at who held it, for how long, and why. The pattern will usually point to one or two changes that account for most of the delay. Often that's missing auto-approval for matched invoices, or a single approver who controls too much volume.

Make those changes first, measure the next close, and build from there. The month-end crunch isn't a law of nature. It's a routing design, and routing can be redesigned.
