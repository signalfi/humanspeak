Every controller knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a manageable pace. Then the calendar flips to the last week of the month and the queue swells. Plant managers stop answering. Purchase orders don't match receipts. Someone in maintenance has forty invoices sitting in their inbox. By day three of close, your team is chasing signatures instead of reconciling accounts.

It's tempting to blame the approvers. But the stall usually isn't a people problem. It's a routing problem, and routing is something finance controls.

## Why the last week is different

**Your approvers are closing their own month.** The operations manager who approves MRO invoices is also finalizing production reports, reconciling scrap, and prepping for the monthly ops review. The plant controller is doing inventory counts. Approval requests land at the moment your approvers have the least attention to give them.

**Invoices arrive in waves.** Many vendors bill at month-end, which means your heaviest invoice volume coincides with your approvers' busiest week. A routing design that works fine at 60 invoices a day falls apart at 200.

**Receiving lags on the plant floor.** In manufacturing, the three-way match depends on someone at the dock logging the receipt. When receiving is behind, and it often is at month-end when shipping is prioritized, invoices fall out of automatic matching and into manual exception queues. Those exceptions then route to approvers who have to figure out whether the goods actually arrived.

**Sequential routing compounds delays.** If an invoice needs a department head, then a plant manager, then a VP, each step waits on the previous one. A one-day delay at each stage becomes a three-day delay overall, and that's before anyone goes on vacation.

**Thresholds were set years ago and never revisited.** Many approval matrices were built when the company was half its size. A $2,500 threshold that made sense in 2015 now sends hundreds of routine invoices to senior managers every month.

## What to change in your approval routing

### 1. Route by exception, not by amount alone

Dollar thresholds are a blunt tool. A $40,000 invoice that matches its PO and receipt exactly carries less risk than a $3,000 invoice with no PO at all. Redesign your routing so that clean, matched invoices within tolerance flow through with minimal touch, and human attention goes to invoices that are unmatched, over tolerance, or from new vendors.

This single change often removes a large share of invoices from approvers' queues entirely. Measure your current match rate first so you know your starting point.

### 2. Replace serial approvals with parallel ones where you can

Not every approver needs to see an invoice after the previous one. If the department head is confirming the work was done and the budget owner is confirming the spend is coded correctly, those checks can happen at the same time. Reserve sequential routing for genuine dependencies, such as when a senior approver needs to see that a manager has already signed off.

### 3. Set escalation timers and move the deadline earlier

An approval request with no deadline will lose to every task that has one. Configure automatic reminders at 24 hours and escalation to a backup approver at 48 hours. Then publish an internal approval cutoff that sits several business days before your actual close deadline. When approvers know invoices received after the 25th still need sign-off by the 28th, behavior changes.

### 4. Build delegation into the calendar, not into panic

Month-end often coincides with travel, audits, and PTO. Require approvers to designate a delegate in the system before any planned absence, and set a default delegate for every approval role so nothing orphans. Chasing a plant manager on a customer visit is not a close activity.

### 5. Fix receiving before you fix approvals

For manufacturers, the fastest way to reduce approval exceptions is upstream. Work with operations to set a same-day receiving standard, especially in the last week of the month. Give dock staff a simple way to log receipts, whether that's a scanner, a tablet, or a mobile workflow, and report receiving lag by location. If receiving is logged, far more invoices match automatically and never need a human approver.

### 6. Accrue what you can't approve

Not every invoice has to be fully approved to close the books. For recurring services and goods you know were received, build a disciplined accrual process so a late approval doesn't hold up the close. Approval can finish in the next period; your financial statements shouldn't wait for it.

### 7. Report approval aging by approver

What gets measured gets managed. Publish a simple weekly report showing open approvals by approver and by age. Most people don't realize they're the bottleneck until they see their name at the top of the list. Share it with operations leadership, not as a shaming exercise but as a shared close metric.

## Start with one month of data

You don't need to redesign everything at once. Pull the last three month-ends and look at where invoices sat longest: which approvers, which invoice types, which exception reasons. Most teams find that a small number of routing rules and a few approvers account for most of the delay. Fix those first.

The goal isn't to remove controls. It's to point human attention at the invoices that actually need it, so your team spends close week closing, not chasing.
