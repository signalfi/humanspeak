# Why Invoice Approvals Stall in the Last Week of the Month, and the Routing Change That Speeds Up Close

It's the 27th. The close calendar says AP cutoff is tomorrow, and 60-odd invoices are still sitting in approval queues. One is waiting on a plant manager who is walking the floor for month-end inventory counts. Another went to the operations director, who forwarded it to a maintenance supervisor, who says it isn't his. A third belongs to a VP who approves everything but hasn't opened the system since the 19th. You'll spend most of today on email, Teams, and walks down the hall, and some of these will still get accrued instead of booked.

If you're a controller at a mid-sized manufacturer, you probably know this week well. When it happens every month, the common response is to adjust approval thresholds. Before you do, it helps to look at why approvals stall, because the cause usually points to a different fix.

## Why the last week is worse than the other three

Several pressures arrive together at month end, and most of them sit outside AP's control.

**Invoice volume bunches up.** Many vendors bill at month end, and receiving often posts in batches. Your queue fills fastest in the days when you have the least time to clear it.

**Your approvers have their own deadlines that week.** Plant managers are closing out production reports and cycle counts. Purchasing is expediting shipments. Engineering is finishing month-end project updates. Approving invoices is the easiest task for them to put off, because nothing on the plant floor stops when they do.

**Invoices go to people who can't evaluate them.** This is the largest cause, and it gets the least attention. An approver who can't tell whether a $4,800 invoice for hydraulic fittings is legitimate won't approve it. They'll hold it, ask someone, or forward it. Each forward restarts the wait.

**Escalations go to the busiest people.** When an invoice ages past a few days, most workflows send it up the chain. At month end, the people higher in the chain are the ones with the least time.

Look at those four together. Only one of them, escalation, has much to do with dollar amounts. The rest concern who receives the invoice and whether that person knows what they're looking at.

## Lever one: tightening dollar thresholds

Tightening thresholds usually means lowering the amount at which an invoice needs a more senior approver, or adding approval tiers. The instinct makes sense. After an audit comment, a duplicate payment, or a surprise in the P&L, adding oversight feels responsible.

For speed, though, it tends to backfire. More senior approvals send more invoices to fewer, busier people, and those invoices arrive exactly when those people have the least time. You've added a step to the route without making anyone more able to judge the invoice. A CFO reviewing a $12,000 tooling invoice usually knows less about it than the tooling engineer who ordered it. The CFO is relying on whoever approved it earlier.

Thresholds manage how much risk one person can commit the company to. That matters. But thresholds address control, not speed. If your problem is a slow close, tightening them will likely make that problem worse.

## Lever two: restructuring approval ownership by cost center

Cost-center ownership routes each invoice to the person who owns the budget it hits and who can confirm whether the goods or services arrived. Maintenance supplies go to the maintenance manager. Freight goes to the logistics lead. The plant 2 utility bill goes to plant 2.

Invoices move faster because the first approver can make a decision. They recognize the vendor, know the order, and know whether the price looks right. The questions that cause holds, like "whose is this?" and "did we receive this?", get answered at the first stop.

This approach also strengthens control. Someone who knows the spend is more likely to notice a wrong quantity or an unexpected price increase than someone several levels up.

## How to tell which problem you have

Your approval history can settle this. Export the last three months of approval data and look at where invoices actually waited.

- If many invoices were **forwarded, reassigned, or returned with questions**, you have an ownership problem. Restructure by cost center.
- If invoices sat with **the correct, knowledgeable approver** and aged because of volume or absence, you have a capacity and delegation problem. Cost-center routing with backups solves it.
- If delays cluster on **high-dollar, non-PO invoices waiting on senior sign-off**, thresholds may be part of the picture. But the fix is probably to take routine spend off those desks, not to add more to them.

At most 200–500 person manufacturers we work with, the first pattern dominates.

## What to change in your routing

**1. Map every cost center to one named owner and one named backup.** The backup needs authority in the same dollar band, so a vacation doesn't send invoices up to the VP by default.

**2. Separate PO-backed spend from everything else.** Invoices that pass a three-way match within tolerance have already been authorized once, through the purchase order. Let them approve automatically, or require a single sign-off. Save human review for non-PO invoices and exceptions.

**3. Make delegation automatic.** If backups only take over after someone sends an email, they won't take over at month end. Tie delegation to out-of-office status or to an aging trigger, such as three business days.

**4. Assign a primary owner for split invoices.** Freight or utilities shared across cost centers should route to one approver, with allocation handled afterward. Parallel approvals from three managers give you three chances to stall.

**5. Add a mid-month sweep.** A hard review around the 15th clears the backlog before week four. That leaves the final days for true month-end volume instead of leftovers.

**6. Show approvers their aging weekly.** A short list of what each approver is holding, sent every Monday, works better than calls from you on the 27th.

## Where thresholds still belong

Keep strict limits on capital spend, new vendors, and non-PO purchases above a meaningful amount. That's where senior review adds judgment, not just delay. Treat thresholds as a second layer on top of cost-center routing, not as your main routing logic.

## The decision

If you have to choose one lever, restructure by cost center first. It fixes the main reason invoices stall, gets decisions from the people best qualified to make them, and lets you target thresholds to where they manage real risk. Lowering thresholds on a routing structure that already stalls will send more invoices into the same backlog.
