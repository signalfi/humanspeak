# Why Invoice Approvals Stall in the Last Week of the Month, and How to Fix the Routing

Every controller at a mid-sized manufacturer knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a manageable pace. Then the last week of the month arrives, and the queue fills up. Plant managers stop answering emails, and the AP team starts sending "just checking in" messages. On the second business day of the next month, someone is still asking whether the freight invoices from the 14th were approved or only accrued.

This isn't a people problem, although it often gets treated as one. It's a routing problem. Most approval workflows were designed around an org chart and never tested against the calendar, so they fail at the moment finance needs them most.

## Why the last week is different

**Your approvers are closing their own books.** The plant manager who approves maintenance and MRO spend is also reconciling production variances and fielding questions about inventory counts. The operations VP is reviewing scrap and overtime numbers. In the week you most need their attention on invoices, they have the least of it. An approval request sitting in an inbox competes with work they will be held accountable for, and it usually loses.

**Three-way match breaks at the receiving dock.** In a manufacturing environment, a large share of invoices should be the easiest to approve: they tie to a purchase order, and the goods were received. But if receiving isn't logged in the ERP promptly, the match fails and the invoice drops into manual review. Receiving backlogs tend to peak late in the month, when shipments arrive ahead of quarter-end or customer deadlines. A clean invoice becomes an exception for reasons that have nothing to do with the invoice.

**Routing sends too much to too few people.** Many approval matrices were built when the company was half its current size. Every invoice over $5,000 goes to the CFO. Every capital-related invoice goes to the COO. As volume grows, the thresholds stay the same, and a handful of senior people become the choke point for hundreds of invoices. When one of them travels during close week, the whole queue waits.

**Sequential chains multiply delays.** An invoice that needs approval from a department head, then a plant manager, then finance waits at each step. If each approver takes a day and a half, the invoice spends almost a week in approval before anyone questions it. In the last week of the month, that week doesn't exist.

**Vendors bill late, too.** Freight carriers, utilities, and contract labor agencies often invoice at or after month-end. These invoices arrive when the queue is already at its fullest, and they're often non-PO, so they need human approval.

## What to change in your approval routing

None of the following requires reorganizing the finance department. They are changes to rules, thresholds, and habits.

### 1. Approve by exception, not by default

If an invoice matches its PO and receipt within an agreed tolerance on price and quantity, it shouldn't need a human approver at all. The approval happened when the PO was issued. Route only true exceptions to people: price variances, quantity mismatches, non-PO spend, and new vendors. For many manufacturers, this step alone takes the majority of PO-backed invoices out of the month-end queue.

Set tolerances deliberately. A common starting point is a small percentage or fixed-dollar variance, whichever is lower. Review what falls outside them each quarter.

### 2. Rebuild your thresholds around current volume

Pull the last six months of approved invoices and count how many each approver handled. If one person is signing off on a disproportionate share, raise their threshold or delegate a tier below them. The goal is to put an approver's attention where their judgment adds value, which means the unusual items rather than the recurring ones.

### 3. Go parallel where you can

If a department head and a plant manager both need to sign off, send the request to both at once rather than in sequence. Reserve sequential routing for cases where the second approver genuinely needs the first approver's decision, such as budget-owner approval before a capital review.

### 4. Build delegation into the workflow, not the calendar

Every approver should have a named backup, and the delegation should switch on automatically when an approval sits beyond a set window, not only when someone remembers to set an out-of-office rule. For month-end specifically, consider a shorter window during the final five business days. An invoice that would wait 72 hours mid-month escalates after 24 at close.

### 5. Fix receiving before you fix AP

Work with operations to set a same-day standard for logging receipts in the ERP, especially in the last two weeks of the month. Put a report in front of plant leadership showing invoices blocked by missing receipts. When operations can see its backlog holding up finance, the dock usually gets faster.

### 6. Move the work earlier in the month

Set an internal cutoff, perhaps the 25th, after which AP stops routing non-urgent, non-PO invoices for approval and accrues them instead. Push approvers toward a daily habit rather than a weekly batch. A ten-minute daily review keeps the queue near zero, while a Friday batch guarantees a pile-up during close week.

### 7. Measure the queue by person, not just in total

A single "average approval time" hides the problem. Track open approvals and days-waiting by approver, and share the report weekly with the approvers themselves. Most people fix their own backlog once they can see it next to their peers'.

## What faster approvals actually buy you

Shorter approval cycles don't just make AP's life easier. They reduce the size and guesswork of your accruals, so fewer adjustments hit the following month. They make early-payment discounts reachable. They also give your controller's team time in close week to analyze results rather than chase signatures.

The last week of the month will always be busy. It doesn't have to be the week your approval process falls apart. Route the routine invoices away from people, set up routing that works without them when they're busy, and keep the exception queue small enough to clear in a day.
