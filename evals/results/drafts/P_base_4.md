# The Last-Week Logjam: Why Invoice Approvals Stall Before Close, and How to Reroute Them

Every controller knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a manageable pace. Then the calendar hits the 24th or 25th and the queue swells. Approvers go quiet, exceptions pile up, and your AP team starts sending the same "just need your sign-off" emails for the third time. By day three of close, you're booking accruals for invoices that are sitting, fully valid, in someone's inbox.

This isn't a discipline problem, and it usually isn't a staffing problem. In most mid-sized manufacturers, it's a routing problem. Month-end stress reveals approval paths that were designed for an average week, and those paths fail when volume and attention move in opposite directions.

## Why the last week breaks

**Volume and attention peak at the same time.** Many suppliers bill at month-end, so invoice volume rises just as your approvers are busiest. In a plant environment, the last week is when production managers push to ship, when inventory counts happen, and when maintenance leads close out work orders. The people you've designated as approvers are doing their real jobs at full intensity. An invoice approval request is the easiest thing for them to defer.

**Sequential chains multiply delay.** A typical routing rule sends an invoice from the requester to the department head, then to the plant manager, then to finance for anything above a threshold. Each handoff adds waiting time, and at month-end each approver is slower. A chain that takes two days mid-month can take seven at close, because the delays stack.

**Thresholds were set once and never revisited.** Dollar limits often date from when the company was half its current size, or from an ERP implementation years ago. The result is that routine, recurring spend, such as raw materials under a blanket PO or the monthly freight bill, gets routed to senior people who add no real review. Those senior people are also the ones most likely to be unavailable at month-end.

**Receiving lags create false exceptions.** In manufacturing, the three-way match (PO, receipt, invoice) is where many invoices stall. The goods arrived, but the receiving entry hasn't been posted because the dock is slammed. The invoice drops into an exception queue that looks like a dispute but is really a data-entry backlog.

**There's no plan for absence.** Approvers travel to supplier sites, take PTO before a holiday, or get pulled into an audit. If delegation depends on someone remembering to set it up, it won't be set up.

## What to change in your approval routing

The goal isn't to make approvers work harder in the last week. It's to make sure fewer invoices need them, and that the ones that do can't get stuck.

### 1. Let matched invoices approve themselves

If an invoice matches its PO and receipt within a defined tolerance, it has already been approved in substance, because someone authorized the purchase when the PO was cut. Configure auto-approval for invoices that pass three-way match within your tolerance (for example, ±2% or a fixed dollar amount, whichever is lower). That removes a large share of PO-backed spend from human queues entirely. Human review should be reserved for exceptions.

### 2. Move from sequential to parallel where you can

Not every approval needs to wait for the one before it. If both the department head and the plant manager must sign off, route to both at once and require both approvals to proceed. This alone can cut days from multi-level chains.

### 3. Re-tier your thresholds based on risk, not habit

Pull twelve months of approval data and look at what senior approvers actually rejected or changed. If the plant manager approved 99% of invoices under $10,000 without comment, that tier is ceremony, not control. Raise it. Keep senior review for non-PO spend, new vendors, capital items, and amounts that are genuinely material. Your auditors will care far more that controls are risk-based and documented than that every invoice touched a VP.

### 4. Make delegation and escalation automatic

Every approver should have a named backup, set in the system rather than in someone's memory. Then add time-based escalation: if an invoice sits for 48 hours (or 24 in the last week of the month), it moves to the backup or the next level up automatically, with a notification to the original approver. Escalation shouldn't read as punishment. It's a guarantee that no single inbox can hold up close.

### 5. Close the receiving gap upstream

If receiving lags are driving your exceptions, the fix lives on the dock, not in AP. Work with operations to set a receiving-entry SLA, such as same shift or within 24 hours, and give receiving clerks a simple mobile or terminal workflow. Then report weekly on exceptions caused by missing receipts. Once operations leaders see the number, it tends to shrink.

### 6. Publish a cut-off calendar and an approval SLA

Tell approvers exactly when invoices must be approved to make the current period, and send a pre-close reminder listing only their open items. A specific, short list ("you have four invoices worth $38,200 pending") gets a far better response than a generic "please clear your queue" email.

### 7. Watch the queue daily, not at close

Build a simple dashboard showing pending approvals by approver, age, and dollar value. Review it daily in the last ten days of the month. The invoices that will cause accrual headaches on day two of close are visible on the 22nd if someone is looking.

## The payoff

When routing is designed for month-end instead of the average week, several things improve together. Close gets shorter because fewer invoices need accruing. Accruals get more accurate because they cover fewer, better-understood items. Suppliers get paid on time, which matters when you're asking them for priority on a rush order. And your AP team stops spending the last week of every month as a collections agency for internal signatures.

None of this requires a massive transformation project. Most of it is configuration and a few conversations with operations. Start by pulling last month's approval data and asking one question: which invoices waited the longest, and why? The answer will tell you which of these changes to make first.
