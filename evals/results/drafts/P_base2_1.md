# The Last-Week Pileup: Why Invoice Approvals Stall at Month-End, and How to Reroute Them

Every controller knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a manageable pace. Then the calendar hits the 24th or 25th, and the approval queue swells. Plant managers stop answering emails. A freight invoice sits in someone's inbox for nine days. By the time your team is trying to close, AP is chasing signatures instead of reconciling, and you're booking accruals you know will need to be reversed.

The month-end stall isn't a discipline problem. It's a routing problem. And at a 200 to 500 person manufacturer, it has causes that are specific to how your business runs.

## Why the Last Week Is Different

**Your approvers are busiest exactly when you need them.** In manufacturing, the last week of the month is when operations pushes hardest to ship. Plant managers, production supervisors, and maintenance leads are on the floor, fighting fires and hitting output targets. Approving a $1,400 MRO invoice is the last thing on their minds. Many companies route approvals to these operational leaders, so the queue freezes at the moment the business is most distracted.

[QUOTE: Controller or VP of Finance at a mid-sized manufacturer describing the tension between operations' month-end shipping push and finance's close timeline. Source from a Ledgerline customer interview, with written approval for attribution.]

**Receiving documentation lags behind invoices.** Three-way match depends on a receipt. When the dock is slammed at month-end, goods get received physically but not entered in the system for days. Invoices that should match automatically land in the exception queue instead, and each one needs a human to investigate. What looks like an approval delay is often a receiving delay.

**Serial approval chains compound every delay.** If an invoice must pass from the requester to the department head to the plant manager to the CFO in sequence, one missing approver stops everything. A two-day delay at each step becomes an eight-day delay overall. At month-end, when several approvers are unavailable at once, serial routing turns small holdups into a backlog.

**Thresholds were set years ago and never revisited.** Many approval matrices were built when the company was half its current size. A $500 threshold for plant manager sign-off may have made sense in 2015. Today it sends hundreds of low-risk invoices a month to someone who rubber-stamps them anyway, usually in a batch on the 28th.

**Nobody owns delegation.** Approvers take vacation, travel to supplier sites, or leave the company. Without automatic delegation rules, invoices wait for someone who isn't coming back soon.

## What to Change in Your Approval Routing

You don't need to rebuild your AP function to fix this. Most of the gains come from a handful of routing changes.

### 1. Route by risk, not by habit

Sort your invoice population into tiers. PO-backed invoices that match receipt and price within tolerance are low risk. Recurring vendor invoices under a set dollar amount, like utilities, leases, and contracted services, are low risk. Non-PO invoices, new vendors, and anything outside tolerance deserve human review.

Then set rules to match. PO-matched invoices within tolerance should post without manual approval. Your approvers already approved the spend when they signed the purchase order. Asking them to approve it again at invoice time adds delay without adding control.

### 2. Switch from serial to parallel approval where you can

When an invoice genuinely needs several approvers, such as a cost center owner and a budget holder, route to both at once instead of in sequence. Reserve serial chains for cases where the second approver truly needs the first approver's judgment. Parallel routing alone can take days off the approval cycle for multi-approver invoices.

### 3. Rebuild the threshold matrix

Pull twelve months of approval data. Look at how often invoices in each dollar band were rejected or questioned. If plant managers approved 99% of invoices under $2,500 without changes, that threshold isn't controlling anything. Raise it, and put the saved attention toward the invoices that actually get questioned.

### 4. Make delegation automatic and escalation timed

Every approver should have a designated backup, and the system should route to that backup when the primary is out of office. Just as important, set escalation timers. If an invoice sits unapproved for 48 hours, it should go to the backup or to the next level up, with a notification. Tighten the timers in the last ten days of the month, when every day counts.

### 5. Route to roles, not names

Assign approvals to positions like "Plant 2 Maintenance Manager" rather than to named individuals. When people change roles or leave, the routing stays intact. This one change removes a large share of orphaned invoices.

### 6. Close the receiving gap

Work with operations to set a receiving standard, such as goods entered within 24 hours of arrival. Give dock staff a simple way to do it, whether that's a handheld scanner or a tablet at the receiving door. Track receiving lag as a metric. Fewer missing receipts means fewer exceptions and faster matching.

### 7. Pull the deadline forward

Set an internal approval cutoff several business days before period end, and communicate it to approvers every month. Invoices received after the cutoff get accrued based on PO and receipt data rather than held for approval. That way the close proceeds on estimates you can defend, instead of waiting on signatures.

### 8. Give approvers a mobile option

A plant manager on the floor won't log into an ERP portal. They will tap "approve" on a phone notification that shows the invoice, the PO, and the match status. Meet approvers where they work.

## Measure the Change

Before you adjust routing, capture a baseline: average approval time, percentage of invoices approved without touch, exception rate, and days to close. Revisit those numbers after two or three close cycles. The goal isn't just a faster close. It's a close where your team spends the last week reconciling and analyzing, not chasing approvals.
