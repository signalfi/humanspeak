# Why Invoice Approvals Stall in the Last Week of the Month, and How to Reroute Them

Every controller knows the pattern. For three weeks, invoices move through approval at a manageable pace. Then close week arrives, and AP is chasing plant managers for signatures, reconciling receiving discrepancies that surfaced days ago, and posting accruals for invoices that are sitting in someone's inbox.

At a 200-to-500-person manufacturer, this is rarely a staffing problem or a discipline problem. It's usually a routing problem. The approval workflow was designed for a smaller company, or it grew by accretion, one exception and one new approver at a time, until it quietly guaranteed a month-end pileup.

Here's why the stall happens and what you can change in your routing to close faster.

## Why approvals bunch up at month-end

### 1. Your approvers aren't desk workers

In manufacturing, many of the people who approve invoices spend their days on the floor, not in email. A maintenance manager signing off on a $4,000 parts invoice may check approvals twice a week. A plant manager may batch them until someone from finance calls. That behavior is rational, because their job is keeping lines running. But it means invoices age silently until close pressure forces the issue.

### 2. Sequential routing multiplies delay

Many workflows send an invoice from the department head to the plant manager to the controller to the CFO, each waiting on the one before. If each approver takes an average of two days to act, a four-step chain takes eight business days before anyone at the end sees it. An invoice received on the 18th can easily miss close.

### 3. Approval thresholds haven't kept up

Dollar thresholds set years ago, when the company was half its current size, push routine spending up the chain. When a $2,500 threshold forces senior review on recurring MRO purchases, your most senior approvers become the bottleneck for your least risky invoices.

### 4. Exceptions go to the wrong people

Three-way match failures, such as quantity variances, missing receipts, or price discrepancies, often land in AP's queue even though AP can't resolve them. The real fix sits with receiving (who hasn't logged the goods receipt) or purchasing (who needs to update the PO price). AP forwards the exception by email, it gets buried, and it resurfaces during close.

### 5. Nobody has a backup

Vacations, plant visits, and sick days don't pause the calendar. Without delegation rules, an invoice assigned to an absent approver simply waits.

## What to change in your approval routing

None of these fixes requires a reorganization. Most can be configured in a quarter.

### Let matched PO invoices skip human approval

The spending decision was already made when the purchase order was approved. If an invoice matches the PO and the receipt within a defined tolerance (for example, ±2% or ±$50, whichever is smaller), there's little value in asking a manager to approve it again. Auto-approving clean matches can remove a large share of your volume from human queues before close week begins.

Set tolerances with your auditors and review them quarterly. The goal is to save human attention for invoices that need it.

### Route in parallel where controls allow

Not every approval needs to be sequential. If both the department head and the project owner must sign off, send the invoice to both at once. Keep sequential routing only where the order matters, such as when a senior approver needs to see that the budget owner has already confirmed.

### Re-tier your thresholds by risk, not only by dollars

Rebuild approval tiers around category and vendor risk as well as amount. Recurring invoices from established vendors under contract (utilities, leases, standing MRO orders) can carry higher single-approver limits. New vendors, one-time capital purchases, and non-PO spend deserve tighter review. This matches oversight to actual risk and takes routine work off senior desks.

### Send exceptions directly to the owner

Configure routing so that a missing receipt goes to the receiving supervisor at the relevant plant, and a price variance goes to the buyer who owns the PO. AP stays informed but stops acting as a relay. Give each exception type a named owner and a response deadline.

### Build delegation and escalation into the workflow

Require every approver to have a designated delegate, and make out-of-office delegation automatic rather than optional. Then add time-based escalation: if an invoice sits untouched for three business days, it goes to the approver's manager or delegate. Escalation shouldn't feel punitive. It's a safety net that keeps one person's busy week from becoming the whole company's close problem.

### Publish a pre-close approval cutoff

Set a clear internal deadline, such as all invoices received by the 22nd approved by the 26th, and share it with approvers each month. Pair it with a mid-month nudge listing each approver's pending items and their age. When approvers know the date and see their own queue, much of the last-minute scramble disappears.

### Make mobile approval the default

For approvers on the plant floor, a mobile approval that takes 30 seconds between shift meetings beats a desktop login they'll get to on Friday. If your current system requires a VPN and a laptop to approve an invoice, that friction shows up in your close calendar.

## Measure the right things

To know whether the changes are working, track a few metrics monthly:

- **Invoice cycle time**, from receipt to approval, broken out by approver and by plant
- **Touchless rate**, the percentage of invoices auto-approved through matching
- **Exception aging**, how long match failures sit before resolution, by exception type
- **Month-end concentration**, the share of monthly invoices approved in the final five business days

The last metric is the clearest signal. If routing is healthy, approvals spread across the month instead of piling into the last week.

## The payoff

Faster approvals do more than shorten close. They reduce accrual estimates and the true-ups that follow, let you capture early-payment discounts that currently expire in someone's queue, and give the controller's team back the days now spent chasing signatures.

The last-week stall isn't inevitable. It's the predictable result of routing rules that no longer fit how your company operates. Change the routing and the bottleneck moves out of your close.
